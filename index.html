Chapter Five

The Behavior of Interest Rates

Interest rates emerge from the bond market — or equivalently, the money market. Anything that shifts the supply or demand for bonds (or money) changes the rate. This chapter is a machine for predicting those shifts.

Concept · 5.1

The Theory of Portfolio Choice

Four factors determine the demand for any asset. Learn them once; use them everywhere.

Wealth
Higher wealth → more demand for all assets, including bonds.
Expected return
Relative to alternatives. If bonds beat stocks on expected return → bond demand ↑.
Risk
Relative to alternatives. Bond risk ↑ → demand ↓ (risk aversion).
Liquidity
Relative to alternatives. Easier to sell → demand ↑.
Concept · 5.2

Shifters of the Bond Market

Any of these shifts the demand curve for bonds. Supply is driven by a different, shorter list.

FactorDemandSupply
Wealth Right—
Expected return on bonds Right—
Bond risk Left—
Bond liquidity Right—
Expected inflation LeftRight
Expected profitability —Right
Government deficit —Right

Rule of thumb: Demand right → price up → rate down. Supply right → price down → rate up.

Concept · 5.3

The Fisher Effect

When expected inflation rises, both sides of the market react. Bond demand falls (lower real return); bond supply rises (cheaper real borrowing). Price drops, rate rises — by approximately the increase in expected inflation.

i ≈ r + πe
Nominal rate = real rate + expected inflation
Click to copy
Concept · 5.4

The Liquidity Preference Framework

An alternative lens: households hold two assets — money and bonds. Interest rates are determined in the money market.

Income effect
Y ↑ → money demand shifts right → i ↑.
Price-level effect
P ↑ → need more nominal $ for same real transactions → Md right → i ↑.
Money supply
Fed OMO purchase → Ms right → i ↓.
Intuition

The interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money. Holding cash means giving up bond yield.

Check yourself
Wealth increases. What happens to interest rates?
Bond demand shifts right → price ↑ → interest rate falls.
Expected inflation rises by 2 percentage points. What happens to nominal rates?
Nominal rates rise by roughly 2 percentage points (Fisher effect). Both bond supply shifts right and demand shifts left → price falls.

Chapter Six

The Risk & Term Structure of Interest Rates

Bonds of the same maturity differ by risk, tax status, and liquidity. Bonds of different maturities form a yield curve whose shape three competing theories try to explain.

Concept · 6.1

Risk Structure

Even at the same maturity, bonds price differently. Three characteristics drive the spread.

Default risk
Chance the issuer fails to pay. Higher risk → higher yield (risk premium).
Liquidity
Less liquid bonds carry a yield premium to compensate for harder resale.
Tax status
Tax-exempt bonds (municipals) offer lower pre-tax yield — same after-tax return.
risk premium = ycorp − yTreasury
Same maturity. Compensation for default risk.
Click to copy
Concept · 6.2

Three Facts the Yield Curve Must Explain

Any credible theory of the term structure has to account for all three of these empirical observations.

#Observation
1Rates on different maturities move together over time.
2When short rates are low, curves tend to slope up. When high, curves tend to invert.
3Yield curves typically slope upward.
Concept · 6.3

Three Theories, Compared

TheoryAssumptionExplains
ExpectationsBonds of all maturities are perfect substitutes. Long rate = avg of expected short rates.Facts 1 & 2
Segmented MarketsBonds of different maturities are not substitutes at all.Fact 3
Liquidity PremiumPartial substitutes; investors prefer short. Long rate = avg expected short rates + term premium.All three
int = (it + iet+1 + ⋯ + iet+n−1) / n + lnt
Liquidity premium theory — n-year rate
Click to copy
Signal

A downward-sloping (inverted) yield curve typically signals markets expect short rates to fall — often ahead of a recession.

Check yourself
Which term-structure theory explains all three yield curve facts?
Liquidity Premium Theory. It combines the expectations theory's average-of-short-rates with a preference for short maturities, so it explains fact 3 (typical upward slope) as well as facts 1 and 2.
If expected one-year rates over the next three years are 3%, 4%, and 5%, what is today's three-year rate under pure expectations theory?
4%. The three-year rate = (3 + 4 + 5) ÷ 3 = 4%. (Add a liquidity premium under LPT.)

Chapter Thirteen

Central Banks & the Federal Reserve System

The Federal Reserve is the central bank of the United States — independent within government, structured to insulate monetary policy from short-term political pressure.

Concept · 13.1

Structure

Board of Governors
7 members, 14-year staggered terms, headquartered in Washington D.C.
12 Reserve Banks
Regional banks. Examine member banks, clear checks, act as lender of last resort.
FOMC
12 voting members — 7 Governors + NY Fed President + 4 rotating Reserve Bank presidents. Meets ~8× / year.
Member banks
All nationally chartered banks; state banks may opt in.
Concept · 13.2

The Dual Mandate

Congress gave the Fed two co-equal primary goals — plus a technical third that gets less attention.

Maximum employment
Push the unemployment rate toward its natural rate.
Price stability
Keep inflation low and predictable. Fed targets ~2%.
Moderate long-term rates
Typically achieved automatically when the first two are met.
Check yourself
How many voting members sit on the FOMC?
Twelve. 7 Governors + NY Fed President (permanent) + 4 rotating Reserve Bank presidents.

Chapter Fourteen

The Money Supply Process

The Fed controls the monetary base. Banks and depositors decide how that base gets multiplied into the broader money supply. The money multiplier ties them together.

Concept · 14.1

Three Players, One Outcome

The Fed
Controls the monetary base via open market operations.
Banks
Decide how much of their reserves to lend out vs. hold as excess.
Depositors
Decide how to split holdings between currency and deposits.
MB = C + R
Monetary base = currency in circulation + total reserves
Click to copy
Concept · 14.2

The Money Multiplier

In a fractional reserve system, one dollar of new base becomes several dollars of money supply. The multiplier ties the two together.

m = (1 + c) / (r + e + c)
Where c = C/D, r = required reserve ratio, e = ER/D
Click to copy
M = m × MB
Money supply = multiplier × monetary base
Click to copy
Variable risesEffect on mWhy
c (currency-deposit ratio)FallsMore leakage into non-multiplying currency.
r (required reserve ratio)FallsBanks must hold more, lend less.
e (excess reserve ratio)FallsBanks hoard reserves instead of lending.
Concept · 14.3

Crises and the Multiplier

Two moments in history where m collapsed and dragged M with it — even as MB moved in the opposite direction.

1929–33
Bank runs pushed c up (people withdrew currency). Fearful banks pushed e up (hoarded reserves). m collapsed → M fell sharply, even with MB roughly stable. A monetary policy failure.
2007–08
Fed expanded MB dramatically. But banks hoarded the injection (e exploded). m fell just as hard — M grew only modestly despite a base multiple times larger.
Check yourself
Suppose r = 0.10, c = 0.40, and e = 0.05. Compute the money multiplier.
m = (1 + 0.40) / (0.10 + 0.05 + 0.40) = 1.40 / 0.55 ≈ 2.55.
Why did the money supply fall in 1929–33 even though the monetary base didn't collapse?
Both c and e spiked — depositors pulled currency (bank runs) and surviving banks hoarded reserves. The money multiplier collapsed, so M = m × MB fell sharply even with MB stable.

Chapter Fifteen

Tools of Monetary Policy

The Fed moves the federal funds rate by acting on the market for reserves. Four tools do most of the work — OMO, discount lending, IOR, and reserve requirements.

Concept · 15.1

The Market for Reserves

The FFR sits at the intersection of a downward-sloping demand for reserves and a vertical supply. Two horizontal segments — floor and ceiling — bound the picture.

Concept · 15.2

How Each Tool Moves the FFR

ToolMechanismEffect on FFR
Open market purchaseInjects reserves — Supply shifts rightFalls
Open market saleDrains reserves — Supply shifts leftRises
Lower discount rateLowers the supply-curve ceilingFalls (if binding)
Raise IORRaises the demand-curve floorRises
Raise reserve requirementBanks need more — Demand shifts rightRises
In Practice

Today the Fed steers the FFR primarily through IOR and the overnight reverse repo rate (ON RRP) — bracketing the rate from above and below. Open market operations remain the most flexible tool.

Concept · 15.3

Why OMO Wins

Among conventional tools, open market operations are preferred because they are:

Fully controlled
Fed decides the exact quantity of reserves added or drained.
Flexible
Any size, from micro-adjustments to major interventions.
Reversible
A purchase today can be undone by a sale tomorrow.
Fast
Same-day execution — no lag between decision and effect.
Check yourself
The Fed conducts an open market purchase. What happens in the market for reserves?
The supply curve shifts right (more reserves injected) → FFR falls.
If the Fed raises IOR above the current FFR, what happens?
The demand curve's floor rises. Banks won't lend below IOR (they can just park at the Fed), so FFR rises to match.

Chapter Sixteen

The Conduct of Monetary Policy

Monetary policy is a chain of causation: tools act on operating instruments, which move intermediate targets, which achieve final goals. The chain is only as strong as its weakest link.

Concept · 16.1

The Policy Chain

LayerExamples
ToolsOMO, discount lending, IOR, reserve requirements
Operating instrumentsReserve aggregates, federal funds rate
Intermediate targetsMonetary aggregates (M1, M2), long-term rates
GoalsMaximum employment, price stability
Concept · 16.2

Two Targeting Strategies

Nonborrowed reserves targeting
Fed pins the quantity of reserves via OMO. The FFR is allowed to float. Delivers stable money growth, volatile rates. Friedman-favored.
FFR targeting
Fed pins the rate; reserves adjust to whatever level clears the market. This is the modern Fed's approach — stable rates, floating reserves.
Check yourself
Under FFR targeting, what floats?
Reserves. The Fed conducts whatever OMO is needed to keep the FFR at target — the reserve quantity moves to clear the market.

Chapter Nineteen

Quantity Theory, Inflation & Money Demand

The classical claim: in the long run, money is neutral. Sustained money growth in excess of output growth becomes inflation. One identity — MV = PY — encodes it.

Concept · 19.1

The Quantity Equation

An identity — always true by construction. Its power comes from what you're willing to assume about velocity.

M × V = P × Y
Level form — money × velocity = price level × real output
Click to copy
%ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY
Growth form — useful for empirical work
Click to copy

Classical assumption: V is stable in the long run. Rearrange: inflation ≈ money growth − output growth. Money is a veil.

Concept · 19.2

Two Views of Money Demand

Quantity theory
Md = (1/V) × P × Y. Money demand depends only on nominal income. No role for the interest rate.
Keynesian — 3 motives
Transactions (function of income) · Precautionary (function of income) · Speculative (function of interest rate, inversely).
The Difference That Matters

Keynes made money demand a function of interest rates — which is why the liquidity preference framework in Ch. 5 exists at all. In Fisher's quantity theory, that channel is muted.

Concept · 19.3

Debt Monetization & Hyperinflation

When a government's deficits are financed by the central bank printing money, the mechanism is direct:

Deficit → CB buys new gov't debt with newly created money → MB ↑ → M ↑ → P ↑.

Persistent debt monetization is the historical recipe for hyperinflation — Weimar Germany, Zimbabwe, mid-2010s Venezuela. The identity does not permit escape.

Check yourself
M grows at 7%, V is constant, Y grows at 2%. Approximate inflation?
%ΔP ≈ %ΔM + %ΔV − %ΔY = 7 + 0 − 2 = 5%.
Which of Keynes's three motives depends on the interest rate?
The speculative motive. Higher interest rates raise the opportunity cost of holding money, so speculative money demand falls when yields rise.

Chapter Twenty-one

Monetary Policy & the Aggregate Demand Curve

The MP curve captures how the Fed sets the real interest rate in response to inflation. Combined with the IS relation, it produces an aggregate demand curve that shifts with policy.

Concept · 21.1

The MP Curve

The Fed's reaction function — the real interest rate r the Fed will set at each level of inflation π. Upward sloping because the Fed raises real rates when inflation rises.

Concept · 21.2

The Taylor Principle

To stabilize inflation, the Fed must raise nominal rates by more than one-for-one with inflation. Only then does the real rate rise, which is what actually cools demand.

Why It Matters

Violate the Taylor principle and monetary policy is destabilizing: higher inflation → lower real rates → more demand → still higher inflation. The 1970s stagflation is the textbook cautionary tale.

Concept · 21.3

Movement vs. Shift

Movement along MP
Automatic response to inflation, given the Fed's rule.
Shift right/down
Autonomous expansionary policy — lower r for any π.
Shift left/up
Autonomous contractionary policy — higher r for any π.
Concept · 21.4

The AD Curve

Downward sloping in (π, Y) space. Higher inflation → Fed raises the real rate (per MP) → C and I fall → equilibrium output Y falls.

ShockAD shift
Autonomous expansionary policyRight
Fiscal expansion (G↑ or T↓)Right
Positive consumer confidence shockRight
Financial crisis / credit contractionLeft
Check yourself
Why does the AD curve slope downward?
Higher π → the Fed raises the real interest rate (MP curve) → consumption and investment fall → equilibrium Y falls. So π and Y are inversely related.
What does the Taylor principle say about nominal interest rates?
The Fed must raise nominal rates more than one-for-one with inflation, so real rates actually rise — otherwise policy is destabilizing.
